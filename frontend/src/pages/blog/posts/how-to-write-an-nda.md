You're about to share your product roadmap with a potential vendor, pitch an investor, or onboard a developer who'll see your entire codebase. Before any of that, you need a **Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA)** — and you need it to actually protect you, not just look official.

This guide walks through writing an NDA from scratch under Indian law. If you'd rather start from a working draft, generate one in two minutes with the [free NDA template](/template/nda) or the [NDA generator](/tools/nda-generator).

> This is general information, not legal advice. For deals involving significant IP or investment, have a lawyer review your NDA.

## Step 1: Choose the type — mutual or one-way

| | One-way (unilateral) NDA | Mutual NDA |
|---|---|---|
| Who discloses | Only one party shares secrets | Both parties share secrets |
| Typical use | Hiring an employee or vendor, pitching | Partnerships, joint ventures, M&A talks |
| Drafting bias | Favours the disclosing party | Balanced obligations |

If both sides will reveal confidential information — common in partnership discussions — use a mutual NDA so the obligations cut both ways. If only you are disclosing, a one-way NDA keeps your obligations minimal.

## Step 2: Identify the parties precisely

Use full legal names: "Acme Technologies Private Limited, CIN U72900KA2020PTC012345, having its registered office at..." — not "Acme" or a founder's personal name when the company owns the secrets. If an individual signs for a company, state that they sign as its authorised signatory.

## Step 3: Define "Confidential Information" carefully

This is the clause most disputes turn on. Define it broadly enough to cover what matters, but specifically enough to be enforceable:

- Business plans, financial data, pricing, customer and vendor lists
- Source code, algorithms, designs, prototypes, technical documentation
- Any information marked confidential, or that a reasonable person would understand to be confidential

Avoid defining *everything* as confidential — Indian courts are reluctant to enforce obligations that are impossibly broad. Pair the broad definition with the standard **exclusions** (Step 5).

## Step 4: State the purpose

An NDA should permit use of the information **only for a stated purpose** — "evaluating a potential software development engagement", for example. Without a purpose clause, the receiving party can argue any use was permitted. This single sentence does a lot of work.

## Step 5: Add the standard exclusions

Information is *not* confidential if it:

1. Is or becomes publicly available through no fault of the receiving party;
2. Was already known to the receiving party before disclosure;
3. Is independently developed without using the confidential information;
4. Is rightfully received from a third party without restriction;
5. Must be disclosed by law or court order (with prompt notice to the discloser).

Courts expect these exclusions; an NDA without them looks oppressive and is harder to enforce.

## Step 6: Set obligations and the standard of care

The receiving party should agree to (a) keep the information confidential, (b) use it only for the purpose, (c) share it internally only on a need-to-know basis, and (d) protect it with at least the same care as its own confidential information, and never less than reasonable care.

## Step 7: Fix the term — and survival

Two different periods matter:

- **Term of the agreement**: how long the NDA operates (commonly 1–3 years).
- **Survival of confidentiality**: how long secrets stay protected after the agreement ends (commonly 2–5 years; trade secrets are often protected indefinitely).

Writing "confidentiality obligations survive termination for three years" avoids the classic gap where obligations die with the agreement.

## Step 8: Remedies, return of information, and governing law

- **Return/destruction**: on request or termination, the receiving party returns or destroys all confidential material and certifies it.
- **Remedies**: state that breach causes irreparable harm for which damages may be inadequate, entitling the discloser to seek **injunctive relief** — in practice, the injunction is the remedy that matters, because quantifying damages for a leaked secret is hard.
- **Governing law and jurisdiction**: "This Agreement is governed by the laws of India; courts at Bengaluru, Karnataka have exclusive jurisdiction" — pick your home state. Arbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 is a common alternative.

## Step 9: Execute it properly

1. Print on plain paper or stamp paper as your state requires — an NDA is valid on plain paper, but nominal stamp duty (often Rs. 100–500 depending on the state) makes it cleanly admissible in evidence. See the [stamp duty guide](/blog/stamp-duty-guide-india).
2. Both parties sign every page, with name, title and date.
3. Exchange fully signed copies — an NDA only you have signed protects nobody.

## Common NDA mistakes to avoid

- **Signing the NDA after disclosure.** Information shared before execution is usually not covered. Sign first, talk second.
- **No purpose clause.** Any use becomes arguable.
- **Everything is confidential.** Overbroad NDAs invite courts to read them narrowly.
- **Perpetual obligations on all information.** Reserve indefinite protection for genuine trade secrets.
- **Ignoring employees and subcontractors.** Bind the receiving party for its people's conduct.
- **Confusing an NDA with a non-compete.** An NDA protects information; it does not stop someone working for a competitor. India treats post-employment non-competes as largely unenforceable under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act — a well-drafted NDA is often your *stronger* protection. ## Are NDAs enforceable in India?

Yes. NDAs are enforceable as contracts under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and courts grant injunctions against misuse of confidential information. Enforcement is strongest when the NDA is specific about what is confidential, the obligations are reasonable in scope and duration, and you can show the information was actually treated as secret. India has no standalone trade-secret statute, so your NDA *is* the primary legal protection — one more reason to draft it well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I write an NDA myself without a lawyer?

Yes. For routine situations — vendors, freelancers, early business discussions — a well-structured template covers the standard clauses. Involve a lawyer when the stakes are high: fundraising, M&A, or core trade secrets.

### How long should an NDA last?

Most business NDAs run 1–3 years, with confidentiality obligations surviving 2–5 years after termination. Genuine trade secrets can be protected for as long as they remain secret.

### Does an NDA need to be notarised or on stamp paper in India?

Neither is required for validity. Nominal stamp duty (state-dependent) makes the document cleanly admissible as evidence, and notarisation adds proof of execution, but an NDA signed on plain paper is still a binding contract.

### What happens if someone breaks an NDA?

The disclosing party can seek an injunction to stop further use or disclosure, claim damages for losses caused, and in some cases pursue criminal remedies (for example under the Information Technology Act for data theft). The injunction is usually the most valuable remedy.

### Is a one-way or mutual NDA better?

Use a one-way NDA when only you disclose confidential information, and a mutual NDA when both sides do. Signing a mutual NDA when only you disclose needlessly binds you; signing a one-way NDA as the discloser when the other side also shares secrets leaves them unprotected and invites renegotiation.
